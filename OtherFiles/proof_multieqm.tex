
By using equations (\ref{lambda}) and (\ref{eq:xi}), function $F$ in (\ref{F}) is represented as
\begin{align*}
F(\beta) & =\frac{1}{2\beta\omega_{s}}\frac{(\beta^{2}\omega_{s}+\omega_{u})(\beta^{2}\omega_{s}+\omega_{\eta})^{2}}{(\beta^{2}\omega_{s}+\omega_{\eta})^{2}-\theta\omega_{u}\beta^{2}\omega_{s}}.
\end{align*}
Denote $a=\frac{\omega_{s}}{\omega_{\eta}}\beta^{2}$ and $h=\frac{\omega_{u}}{\omega_{\eta}}$ so that the fixed-point problem $\beta = F(\beta)$ becomes
\begin{equation}
    a=\frac{1}{2}\frac{(a+h)(a+1)^{2}}{(a+1)^{2}-\theta ha}.\label{eq:fpp}
\end{equation}

This problem is further reduced to the following cubic equation of $a$:
\begin{align}
0 & =G(a)\nonumber \\
 & =a^{3}+(2-2\theta h-h)a^{2}+(1-2h)a-h.\label{eq:G}
\end{align}
Note that we focus on the solution to (\ref{eq:G}) in $a\ge0$. As $G(0)=-h<0$ and $\lim_{a\rightarrow\infty}G(a)=\infty$, it has at least one solution. 

Taking the first-order derivative of $G$ with respect to $a$,
\[
g(a)\equiv G'(a)=3a^{2}+2(2-2\theta h-h)a+1-2h.
\]
Consider a solution to $g(a)=0$. If $2h>1$, then $g(a)=0$ has a unique solution, meaning that $G(a)$ takes a U-shaped curve. Therefore, there is a unique solution to $G(a)=0$.

Suppose that $2h<1$. Then, the determinant of $g$ is
\begin{align*}
D_{g} & =4\left[4\theta^{2}h^{2}-4h(2-h)\theta+(1+h)^{2}\right].
\end{align*}
Thus, $g(a)=0$ has two solutions in $a\ge0$ if the following conditions are satisfied:
\begin{equation}
4\theta^{2}h^{2}-4h(2-h)\theta+(1+h)^{2}>0\label{eq:cond1}
\end{equation}
 and 
\begin{equation}
2-2\theta h-h<0.\label{eq:cond2}
\end{equation}
Otherwise, $g(a)>0$ holds for all $a\ge0$, and $G(a)$ is monotonically increasing in $a$. In this case, $G(a)=0$ has a unique solution. 

Suppose that conditions (\ref{eq:cond1}) and (\ref{eq:cond2}) are satisfied and denote the solutions of $g(a)=0$ as $a_{1}$ and $a_{2}(>a_{1})$. These solutions, $a_{1}$ and $a_{2}$, represent the tipping points of $G(a)$, such that, $G(a)$ increases with $a$ in $a\le a_{1}$, decreases in $a_{1}<a\le a_{2}$, and increases in $a_{2}<a$. In this case, $G(a)=0$ has three solutions if $G(a_{1})>0$ and $G(a_{2})<0$, and we denote them as $a_{L},a_{M},$ and $a_{H}$ ($a_{L}<a_{M}<a_{H}$). If $G(a_{1})>0$ and $G(a_{2})>0$, then $G(a)=0$ has a unique solution that corresponds to $a_{L}$, while $G(a_{1})<0$ and $G(a_{2})<0$ imply that $G(a)=0$ has a unique solution that corresponds to $a_{H}$. Applying $g(a_{k})=0$ ($k=1,2$), it holds that 
\begin{align*}
G(a_{k}) & =2a_{k}\left[3(1-2h)-(2-2\theta h-h)^{2}\right]-(2-2\theta h-h)(1-2h)-9h
\end{align*}
Hence, $G(a_{1})>0$ and $G(a_{2})<0$ are, respectively, equivalent to
\[
a_{1}<\hat{a}\equiv\frac{\theta h(1-2h)-(1+h)^{2}}{(1+h)^{2}-4\theta h\left(2-h-\theta h\right)}<a_{2}.
\]
These conditions are equivalent to $g(\hat{a})<0$, where
\[
g(\hat{a})=\bar{g}R,
\]
with $\bar{g}>0$ and 
\[
R\equiv2h^{3}(1+2\theta)^{2}+2h^{2}(3-10\theta)+h(6-\theta)+2.
\]
Note that $g(\hat{a})<0$ is a stronger condition than conditions (\ref{eq:cond1}) and (\ref{eq:cond2}). Hence, $G=0$ has three solutions if $R<0$. By rewriting $R$ as a function of $\theta$, we obtain 
\[
R(\theta)=8h^{3}\theta^{2}+\theta h(8h^{2}-20h-1)+2(1+h)^{3}<0.
\]
Taking $R$ as a quadratic function of $\theta$, its determinant is represented as 
\[
D_{R}=-h^{2}(8h-1)^{3}.
\]
 If $h>\frac{1}{8}$ (i.e., $\omega_{\eta}<8\omega_{u}$), then $D_{R}<0$, meaning that $R>0$ holds for all $h>0$. Hence, $G(a)$ is monotonically increasing in $a$ and the solution is unique. If $h<\frac{1}{8}$(i.e., $\omega_{\eta}>8\omega_{u})$, then $D_{R}>0$ and $8h^{2}-20h-1<0$ (due to $8h<1$). Thus $R$ has two solutions, denoted as $\theta_{L}$and $\theta_{H}$, such that $R<0$ if and only if $\theta\in[\theta_{L},\theta_{H}]$. That is, if $\theta$ lies between $\theta_{L}$ and $\theta_{H}$, then $G(a)=0$ has three solutions. If $\theta<\theta_{L}$, then $G(a_{1})>0$ and $G(a_{2})>0$, meaning that the solution is unique at $a_{L}$. If $\theta>\theta_{H}$, then $G(a_{1})<0$ and $G(a_{2})<0$, and the solution is unique at $a_{H}$.

Denote the LHS of (\ref{eq:cond1}) as $L(\theta)$. $L(\theta)=0$ has two solutions $\theta_{1}$ and $\theta_{2}$, while (\ref{eq:cond2}) is rewritten as $\theta>\hat{\theta}\equiv\frac{2-h}{2h}$. It holds that $\theta_{1}<\hat{\theta}<\theta_{2}$. Thus, if $\theta>\theta_{2}$ and $\theta$ approaches to $\theta_{2}$ from above, (\ref{eq:cond1}) is violated, while condition (\ref{eq:cond2}) is satisfied. At $\theta=\theta_{2}$, $D_{g}=0$, meaning that $a_{1}=a_{2}.$ At this point 
\[
a_{1}=a_{2}=a_{0}\equiv-\frac{2-2\theta_{2}h-h}{3}>0,
\]
and $\hat{a}\rightarrow\infty$. Therefore, as $\theta\searrow\theta_{2}$, $a_{1}<a_{2}<\hat{a}$ hold. This implies that $G(a_{1})>0$ and $G(a_{2})>0$. The opposite argument holds when $\theta<\hat{\theta}$ and $\theta$ approaches $\hat{\theta}$ from below. Hence, for large $\theta,$ the unique solution corresponds to $a_{H}$, and vice versa.

Suppose that $h<\frac{1}{8}$. To explore the comparative statics of $\theta_{L}$ and $\theta_{H}$ with respect to $h$, consider
\begin{align*}
h\frac{\partial R}{\partial h} & =2\theta h(1+10h)-6(1+h)^{2}.
\end{align*}
Denote 
\[
\theta_{0}=\frac{3(1+h)^{2}}{h(1+10h)},
\]
so that $\frac{\partial R}{\partial h}\gtrless0\Leftrightarrow\theta\gtrless\hat{\theta}_{0}$. At $\theta=\hat{\theta}_{0}$, $R(\hat{\theta}_{0})<0$  due to $h<\frac{1}{8}$. Therefore, $\theta_{L}<\hat{\theta}_{0}<\theta_{H}$, and
\[
\frac{\partial R(\theta_{L})}{\partial h}<0,\ \frac{\partial R(\theta_{H})}{\partial h}>0.
\]
 By the implicit function theorem, for both $k=L$ and $H$,
\[
\frac{d\theta_{k}}{dh}=-\frac{\partial R(\theta_{k})/\partial h}{\partial R(\theta_{k})/\partial\theta}<0.
\]
Consider the limits at $h\rightarrow\frac{1}{8}$. By solving $R=0$, $\theta_{k}=\hat{\theta}_{0}\rightarrow \theta_0 =\frac{27}{2}.$ Also, at $h\rightarrow0$, 
\begin{align*}
\lim_{h\rightarrow0}\theta_{k} & =-\frac{1}{16}\frac{-40\pm24}{\lim_{h\rightarrow0}6h}\\
 & =\infty.
\end{align*}
Hence, we have established that, $\theta_H, \theta_L,$ and $\hat{\theta}_{0}$ are strictly decreasing in $h$ and, therefore, increasing in $\omega_{\eta}$. By considering a unique mapping between $\theta_{k}$ and $\omega_{\eta}$, we define $\omega_H$ as a unique solution to $\theta = \theta_L(\omega_{\eta})$, $\omega_L$ as a unique solution to $\theta = \theta_H(\omega_{\eta})$  and  $\omega_0$ as a unique solution to $\theta = \hat{\theta}_{0}(\omega_{\eta})$.
