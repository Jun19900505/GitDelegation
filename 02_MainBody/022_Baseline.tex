\section{Model}\label{sec:Model}
%===========================================
This section presents a one-period trading model
à la \citet{kyle1985continuous} with delegated investments. The economy consists of a \textit{fund manager}, a competitive \textit{market maker}, \textit{noise traders}, and $n\ge2$ \textit{investors (fund clients)}. All players are risk neutral. The manager leverages her skill (defined later) to discover a single risky asset with potential mispricing, raises funds from investors, and trades the asset with the market maker who set the price $p$. The remaining funds are allocated to risk-free savings with a zero interest rate.\footnote{We abstract away from information acquisition by individual investors. Economies of scale would support this assumption as a natural equilibrium outcome, in which investors do not acquire the necessary skills and information, but rather remain uninformed and invest through a manager (e.g., \citealp{garleanu2018efficiently}). They do not directly participate in the market due to the lack of informational advantages over the market maker.}

\paragraph{\normalfont{\textit{Skill.}}}

The manager can uncover more information about the asset payoff than
the market maker, and we interpret it as her skill as follows. At the beginning of the period, the manager draws private information $s$ from a normal distribution, $N(0,\omega_{s})$. Leveraging $s$, she identifies an asset whose payoff is $\delta = \bar{\delta} + s$ with a publicly know mean payoff $\bar{\delta}$. Since the market maker does not observe $s$, the prior mean of $\delta$ from her perspective is $\mathrm{E}[\delta]=\bar{\delta}+\mathrm{E}[s]=\bar{\delta}$ and is biased by $s$ from the manager's perspective. As the market maker would set a price $p=\mathrm{E}[\delta]=\bar{\delta}$ in the absence of traders, $s$ can be viewed as the asset's ``potential mispricing.''\footnote{$s$ is not the \textit{actual} mispricing because the market maker will take into consideration the presence of the informed manager; thus, $p$ will deviate from $\bar{\delta}$ in equilibrium.} Indeed, as shown later, the manager profits by buying the asset if $s>0$ (underpriced) and selling it if $s<0$ (overpriced); the larger the $|s|$, the larger the profit. In light of this, $|s|$ is referred to as the manager's asset-selection \textit{skill}.\footnote{This formalization of skill aligns with findings in the empirical studies that attribute fund managers' performance to stock-picking skills (e.g., \citealp{wermers2000mutual}; \citealp*{kosowski2006can}).}
 %with $|s|$Moreover, the variance $\mathrm{Var}[s]=\omega_{s}$ controls the shape of the underlying distribution of $s$: a large $\omega_{s}$ represents a wider scope, making the manager more likely to identify an asset with a large mispricing, $|s|$. In light of this, $\omega_{s}$ is referred to as the manager's asset-selection \textit{skill} with $|s|$ being its realized value. 
%In practical terms, $\omega_{s}$ can reflect factors such as the manager's investment in higher education in finance and economics, which broadens the range of information available to her, while $|s|$ captures the realized ability to leverage such investments into superior decision-making in financial markets. In what follows, when there is no risk of confusion, we may refer to $|s|$ simply as ``skill" without explicitly calling it ``realized skill."

\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Investment.}}}

We model the manager's fundraising and trading decisions as a simultaneous game with rational expectations. On the one hand, the manager decides on the investment into the risky asset, anticipating the reactions of her clients and the market maker. This action is represented by a market order, $x$, for the risky asset sent to the market maker. In the financial market, noise traders also submit random and exogenous order $u\sim N(0,\omega_{u})$. The market maker observes the aggregate order flow, $x+u$, and sets the semi-strong efficient price to earn zero expected profit:\footnote{Assume that, on the equilibrium path, one competitive market maker trades the asset. As in \citet{kyle1985continuous}, competitively many market makers exist off the equilibrium path, ensuring a break-even price in the equilibrium.} 
\begin{equation}
p=\mathrm{E}[\delta|x+u].\label{p}
\end{equation}
Moreover, in fundraising, the manager disseminates a noisy signal about her trading strategy to clients, $f=x+e$, where $e$ represents a normally distributed noise, $e\sim N(0,\omega_{e})$.\footnote{The fund manager may deviate \textit{ex-post} from the strategy initially communicated to clients. Such incentives, as well as the role of monitoring in preventing deviations, are analyzed in Section \ref{sec:endogenousopacity}.}
This signal is delivered through private communication between the manager and her clients and could capture the fund manager’s practice of communicating a noisy signal about her strategy, such as high-conviction or concentrated risky position, to investors to attract capital, consistent with the empirical evidence and theoretical rationale in \citet*{anton2021best} and \citet{van2010information}.
The signal is informative about the manager's skill and the return of the fund, and fund clients use it in deciding on capital allocations. 
More precisely, upon observing the fund information, $f=x+e$, investor $i$ allocates $m_{i}$ dollars to the fund, so that the fund's asset under management (AUM) becomes $M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}$. The fund's fee structure is proportional to the total profits: the manager takes $\phi$ fraction of the total fund profit, while the remaining $1-\phi$ fraction is distributed to individual clients according to their contributions to the fund, $m_{i}/M$.\footnote{Introducing a fixed fee does not change our qualitative results, as long as the performance-based fee also exists, $0<\phi<1$.} 

\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Fund opacity.}}}
The noise variance of the fund information, $\mathrm{Var}[e] = \omega_{e}$, is interpreted as the \textit{opacity} of the fund. It could arise from strategic obfuscation, whereby the fund deliberately increases its complexity and obscures the investment strategy to hinder uninformed clients from accurately inferring its true actions. In what follows, we first consider the baseline model with a perfectly transparent fund ($\omega_{s}=0$), while Section \ref{sec:opaque fund} deals with general opacity ($\omega_{s}>0)$. Section \ref{sec:endogenousopacity} considers the manager's strategic choice of fund opacity, where the investors' participation, $n$, also endogenously responds to $\omega_{e}$. It uncovers the cost and benefit of an opaque fund, providing implications for equilibrium investment strategies and financial market quality.

\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Maximization problem.}}} 

The trading profit from the risky investment is represented by $\pi\equiv(\delta-p)x$ so that the total fund proceeds are
\begin{equation}
    y=\pi+M.
\end{equation}
The fund manager maximizes her fee income by controlling her investment strategy:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{x} \mathrm{E}\left[\phi y |s\right].\label{eq:managerproblem}
\end{equation}
Conversely, investor $i$ maximizes her return from delegated investment by controlling her fund contribution:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{m_i}\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{m_i}{M}(1-\phi)y -m_{i}|x+e\right].\label{eq:investorproblem}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Definition of equilibrium.}}} Let $p(x+u)$ represent the market maker's pricing strategy, $x(s)$ denote the manager's trading strategy, and $\{m_{i}(x+e)\}_{i=1}^n$ denote investors' fund allocations. An equilibrium consists of $p(\cdot)$, $x(\cdot)$, and $\{m_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^n$ such that $p(\cdot)$ satisfies \eqref{p} given $x(\cdot)$, $x(\cdot)$ solves (\ref{eq:managerproblem}) given $p(\cdot)$ and $\{m_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^n$, and each $m_i(\cdot)$ solves (\ref{eq:investorproblem}) given $p(\cdot)$ and $x(\cdot)$.

\section{Equilibrium} \label{sec:eqm}


In this section, we search for a linear equilibrium under transparency ($\omega_{e}=0$), in which the manager's market order $x$ is linear in her skill, $s$, while the market maker's pricing strategy, $p$, is also linear in the order flow, $x+u$. Proofs for the analytical results are provided in the \hyperref[app:appendix]{Appendix}.

%===========================================
\subsection{Trading Strategy} \label{ss:conjecture}
Conjecture and later verify that the manager's equilibrium trading strategy is
\begin{equation}
 x(s)=\beta s,\label{x_conj}
\end{equation}
where $\beta>0$ is an endogenous constant determined in the equilibrium. Conjecture \eqref{x_conj} states that the manager buys (sells) the asset if her $s$ is positive (negative), where the order size is proportional to her skill $|s|$; the more skilled the manager, the more aggressively she trades.\footnote{This conjecture follows from the original Kyle model where an informed trader trades based on the difference between her belief and that of the market maker, i.e., $ \mathrm{E}[\delta|s]-\mathrm{E}[\delta] = s$.} The scale factor $\beta$ is referred to as the {\it trading intensity}, representing how intensively the manager exploits her informational advantage over the market maker.

%===========================================
\subsection{Execution Price} \label{ss:price}
The market maker decides on the price of the asset by extracting information about the asset's payoff from the aggregate order flow, $x+u$. Anticipating the trading strategy \eqref{x_conj}, the price in equation \eqref{p} can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
p=\bar{\delta}+\lambda(x+u),\label{eq:p}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\lambda\equiv \frac{\beta \omega_{s}}{\beta^{2} \omega_{s}+\omega_{u}}\label{lambda}
\end{equation}
is ``Kyle's lambda,'' a measure of the price impact of order flow.\footnote{From \eqref{p}, we have $p=\mathrm{E}[\delta|x+u]=\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{E}[\delta|x+u,s]|x+u]=\mathrm{E}[\bar{\delta}+s|x+u]$. Since the market maker believes that the manager follows \eqref{x_conj}, $s$ and $x+u$ are both normally distributed from the market maker's perspective. Thus, $p=\bar{\delta}+\mathrm{E}[s]+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}[s,x+u]}{\mathrm{Var}[x+u]}(x+u-\mathrm{E}[x+u])=\bar{\delta}+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}[s,\beta s+u]}{\mathrm{Var}[\beta s+u]}(x+u-\mathrm{E}[\beta s+u])=\bar{\delta}+\frac{\beta \omega_{s}}{\beta^{2} \omega_{s}+\omega_{u}}(x+u)$.} Following the literature, we define a market to be {\it liquid} if $\lambda$ is small.

%===========================================
\subsection{Delegation Decision} \label{ss:delegation}
By applying the total fund proceeds, $y = \pi + M$, investor $i$'s optimization problem in (\ref{eq:investorproblem}) is rewritten as 
\begin{equation}
    \max_{m_i} \frac{m_i}{M}(1-\phi)\mathrm{E}\left[\pi|f\right]-\phi m_i.\label{eq:mi_max}
\end{equation}
The cost of delegating capital management arises from the fee payment and is linear in the dollar value of the capital supply, $m_i$, as represented by the second term of (\ref{eq:mi_max}). In contrast, the marginal benefit is diminishing in $m_i$, as the fund's proceeds are allocated to clients on a pro-rata basis. This structure ensures the second-order condition of the problem (\ref{eq:mi_max}), and the first-order condition leads to the optimal capital allocation as follows:
\begin{equation}
    m_{i} = \left(\frac{1-\phi}{\phi}\mathrm{E}[\pi|f]\sum_{j\neq i}m_j\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}-\sum_{j\neq i}m_j.\label{eq:mi}
\end{equation}

We focus on the symmetric equilibrium where all investors provide the same amount of capital to the fund. By imposing $m_i = m_j$ for all $i,j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}$, equation (\ref{eq:mi}) is reduced to 
\begin{equation}
    m = \frac{n-1}{n^2}\frac{1-\phi}{\phi}\mathrm{E}[\pi|f].\label{eq:symmetricm}
\end{equation}
It suggests that the total asset under management (AUM) of the fund is 
\begin{equation}
    M \equiv nm = \frac{n-1}{n}\frac{1-\phi}{\phi}\mathrm{E}[\pi|f].\label{eq:M}
\end{equation}
Individual and the total fund provisions are proportional to the expected trading profits conditional on the fund information, $\mathrm{E}[\pi|f]$. This is a natural consequence of the performance-based fee structure, as it makes the per-unit return of fund delegation proportional to the trading profit. By incorporating the trading strategy in (\ref{x_conj}), each client seeks to back out the realized skill level $|s|$ of the manager from fund information, $x+e$, and applies it to compute the expected fund profits. When the fund information is perfectly transparent ($\omega_{e}=0$), clients infer $s = x/\beta$, leading to
\begin{equation}
      \mathrm{E}\left[\pi|x+e\right] = (1-\lambda \beta)\beta\mathrm{E}[s^2|s=x/\beta]=\frac{1-\lambda\beta}{\beta}x^2.\label{eq:exp_pi_transparent}
\end{equation}
Intuitively, both the fund's trading quantity, $x$, and the profit margin, $\delta-p$, are proportional to the skill level in expectation, thereby shaping the fund return, $\pi=(\delta-p)x$, into a quadratic function of $s$, as the first equality in (\ref{eq:exp_pi_transparent}) attests. As the transparent fund information indicates that $s=x/\beta$, the conditional expected profit becomes quadratic in $x$. This result implies that the delegated fund management may generate an additional incentive for the manager to control her trading strategy, $x$, because it influences the total fund flow by making her appear skilled.

Finally, equation (\ref{eq:M}) defines the coefficient of fund flow as
\begin{equation}
    \theta \equiv \frac{n-1}{n}\frac{1-\phi}{\phi},\label{eq:theta}
\end{equation}
which we call the \textit{flow-performance sensitivity} in the following discussion. $\theta$ increases with the number of investors ($n$), as a larger pool of clients contributes more total delegation despite smaller individual supply. It decreases with a higher fee ($\phi$), as it raises the cost of delegation and discourages investment.



%===========================================
\subsection{Manager's Optimization} \label{ss:optimization}
By incorporating the total fund flow ([\ref{eq:M}] and [\ref{eq:exp_pi_transparent}]), the manager's problem in (\ref{eq:managerproblem}) is re-written as
\begin{equation}
    \max_{x} \phi\left[sx - \lambda x^2   + \theta\frac{1-\lambda\beta}{\beta}x^2\right].\label{eq:managerproblem_transparent}
\end{equation}
The first two terms represents the fee income from the trading profit and are essentially identical to the trading profit in the original \citet{kyle1985continuous} model. The third term arises due to the fund inflow and is determined by the quadratic size of risky investments. The optimal trading strategy involves
\begin{equation}
    x = \frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta}{\lambda\beta - \theta(1-\lambda\beta)}s.\label{eq:x=Fbeta}
\end{equation}
This strategy is consistent with our guess in (\ref{x_conj}) when the following condition holds: 
\begin{equation}
    \beta = \frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta}{\lambda\beta - \theta(1-\lambda\beta)}.\label{eq:fixedpoint-transparent}
\end{equation}
Adopting the reaction of the price impact, $\lambda$, in equation (\ref{lambda}), the solution to (\ref{eq:fixedpoint-transparent}) is summarized by the following lemma:
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:transparent}
    When the fund information is transparent ($\omega_{e}=0$), the equilibrium trading strategy and the pricing rules are specified by 
    \begin{equation}
        \beta = \sqrt{(1+2\theta)\frac{\omega_{u}}{\omega_{s}}},\ \lambda = \frac{1}{2(1+\theta)}\sqrt{(1+2\theta)\frac{\omega_{s}}{\omega_{u}}}.\label{eq:eqm_trade_transparent}
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
%As is evident from problem (\ref{eq:managerproblem_transparent}), the flow-performance sensitivity, $\theta$, captures the manager's additional incentive to inflate her risky investment. 
The equilibrium with $\theta=0$ is identical to the original \citet{kyle1985continuous} model, where the manager faces a tradeoff in deciding on her trading intensity: while she seeks to exploit her informational advantage by trading more intensively, it reveals information to the market maker and raises the price impact, cutting into the trading profit. Without fund delegation ($\theta=0$), the equilibrium $\beta$ is determined to balance these competing effects. Conversely, $\theta>0$ brings about the additional risk-taking incentive, because inflating $x$ benefits the manager by making her appear highly skilled and inducing a larger fund flow (see equations [\ref{eq:M}] and [\ref{eq:exp_pi_transparent}]). Since the flow-performance sensitivity, $\theta$, governs the reaction of the fund flow to the performance expectation, equilibrium $\beta$ becomes an increasing function of $\theta$. Conversely, the price impact, $\lambda$, decreases with $\theta$, as the information is ``exaggerated'' in that the order size is too large relative to the true $|s|$. Thus, the market maker attempts to partially ``undo'' the manager's information revelation by incorporating less of it into the price, resulting in a weaker price impact.\footnote{This is because $\lambda$ represents the price's reaction to the order flow, $x+u$, rather than its reaction to the private information, $s$. The latter reaction is measured by $\beta\lambda$ and is monotonically increasing in $\beta$ and $\theta$.}

Our benchmark result in Lemma \ref{lem:transparent} isolates the fundamental mechanism through which the delegated fund management enhances the manager’s risk taking behavior. The tendency of fund managers to ``show off” their skill to attract capital is well discussed in the literature (e.g., \citealp{metrick2010economics}; \citealp*{chung2012pay}; \citealp{barber2017interim}), raising regulational concerns that fund managers have incentives to exaggerate the performance or quality of their funds. Although our benchmark model with a transparent fund clearly generates the show-off incentives described above, it is important to ask whether this is a robust implication. In particular, as emphasized by \citet{kyle1985continuous}, a skilled manager may also have incentives to conceal her skill from the market maker, suggesting a fundamental tension between showing off and hiding. The next section considers a more general model with opacity and shows that, contrary to concerns raised by the literature and by policy makers, the manager may in fact prefer to hide her skill from \textit{both} her clients and the market.
