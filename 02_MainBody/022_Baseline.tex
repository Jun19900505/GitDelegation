\section{Model}\label{sec:Model}
%===========================================
This section presents a one-period trading model
à la \citet{kyle1985continuous} with delegated investments. The economy consists of a risk-neutral \textit{fund manager}, competitive risk-neutral \textit{market makers}, \textit{noise traders}, and $n\ge2$ risk-neutral \textit{investors}. The manager leverages her skill (defined later) to discover a single risky asset with potential mispricing, raises funds from investors, and trades the asset with market makers who set the price $p$. The remaining funds are allocated to risk-free savings with a zero interest rate.\footnote{We abstract away from information acquisition by individual investors. Economies of scale would support this assumption as a natural equilibrium outcome, in which investors do not acquire the necessary skills and information, but rather remain uninformed and invest through a manager (e.g., \citealp{garleanu2018efficiently}). They do not directly participate in the market due to the lack of informational advantages over market makers.}

\paragraph{\normalfont{\textit{Skill.}}}

The manager can uncover more information about the asset payoff than
market makers, and we interpret it as her skill as follows. At the beginning of the period, the manager draws a number $s$ from a normal distribution, $N(0,\omega_{s})$, where $s$ is her private information. Leveraging $s$, she identifies an asset whose payoff is $\delta = \bar{\delta} + s$ with $\bar{\delta}$ representing a publicly known mean payoff. Since market makers do not observe $s$, the prior mean of $\delta$ from their perspective is $\mathrm{E}[\delta]=\bar{\delta}+\mathrm{E}[s]=\bar{\delta}$, which is biased by $s$ from the manager's perspective. As market makers would set a price $p=\mathrm{E}[\delta]=\bar{\delta}$ in the absence of traders, $s$ can be viewed as the asset's ``potential mispricing.''\footnote{$s$ is not the \textit{actual} mispricing because market makers will take into consideration the presence of the informed manager; thus, $p$ will deviate from $\bar{\delta}$ in equilibrium.} Indeed, as shown later, the manager profits by buying the asset if $s>0$ (underpriced) and selling it if $s<0$ (overpriced); the larger the $|s|$, the larger the profit. Thus, we refer to $|s|$ as the manager's \textit{realized} asset-selection skill.\footnote{This formalization of skill aligns with findings in the empirical studies that attribute fund managers' performance to stock-picking skills (e.g., \citealp{wermers2000mutual}; \citealp*{kosowski2006can}).} 

Moreover, the variance of the realized skill, $\mathrm{Var}[s]=\omega_{s}$,
controls the shape of the underlying distribution from which the manager
draws $s$. A large $\omega_{s}$ represents a distribution with a wider scope, making the manager more likely to acquire a high realized skill level, $|s|$. Therefore, we refer to $\omega_{s}$ as the manager's \textit{skill}. In practical terms, $\omega_{s}$ can reflect factors such as the manager's
investment in higher education in finance and economics, which broadens the range of skills available to her. 

\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Investment.}}}

We model the fundraising and trading stages as a simultaneous game with rational expectations. On the one hand, the manager decides on the investment into the risky asset, anticipating the investors' and market makers' reactions, and places a market order for $x$ units to market makers. In the financial market, noise traders also submit random order $u\sim N(0,\omega_{u})$ exogenously. Market makers observe the aggregate order flow, $x+u$, and set the semi-strong efficient price to earn zero expected profit, that is,\footnote{Assume that, on the equilibrium path, one competitive market maker trades the asset. As in \citet{kyle1985continuous}, competitively many market makers exist off the equilibrium path, ensuring a break-even price in the equilibrium.} 
\begin{equation}
p=\mathrm{E}[\delta|x+u].\label{p}
\end{equation}
In raising funds from investors, the manager disseminates a noisy signal about her position to investors, $x+e$, where $e$ represents noise that follows a normal distribution $e\sim N(0,\omega_{e})$. This signal is delivered through private communication between the manager and her customer investors and conveys imperfect information about the fund's prospective risky holdings. It is informative about the manager's skill and the return of the fund, and investors use it in deciding on fund allocations. We interpret $\omega_{e}$ as the \textit{opacity} of the fund. We first consider the case with a perfectly transparent fund ($\omega_{s}=0$) as our baseline model, while Section XXX deals with general opacity ($\omega_{s}>0)$. Section XXX endogenizes $\omega_{s}$ through the manager's choice, e.g., she may obfuscates information by making trading strategies complex and incomprehensible to uninformed investors. Upon observing the fund information, $x+e$, investor $i$ allocates $m_{i}$ dollars to the fund. In aggregate, the fund's asset under management (AUM) becomes $M=\sum_{i=1}^{n}m_{i}$. The fund's fee structure is proportional to the total profits: the manager takes $\phi$ fraction of the total fund profit, while the remaining $1-\phi$ fraction is distributed to individual investors according to their contributions to the fund, $m_{i}/M$.\footnote{Introducing a fixed fee does not change our qualitative results, as long as the performance-based fee also exists, $0<\phi<1$.} 


\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Maximization problem.}}} 

With the trading profit from the risky investment being $\pi\equiv(\delta-p)x$, the total fund proceeding is expressed as
\begin{equation}
    y=\pi+M.
\end{equation}
The fund manager maximizes her fee income by controlling her investment strategy:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{x} \mathrm{E}\left[\phi y \right|s].\label{eq:managerproblem}
\end{equation}
Conversely, investor $i$ maximizes her return from investment by controlling her fund contribution:
\begin{equation}
    \max_{m_i}\mathrm{E}\left[\frac{m_i}{M}(1-\phi)y -m_{i}|x+e\right].\label{eq:investorproblem}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Definition of equilibrium.}}} Let $p(x+u)$ represent market makers' pricing strategy, $x(s)$ denote the manager's trading strategy, and $\{m_{i}(x+e)\}_{i=1}^n$ denote investors' fund allocations. An equilibrium consists of $p(\cdot)$, $x(\cdot)$, and $\{m_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^n$ such that $p(\cdot)$ satisfies \eqref{p} given $x(\cdot)$, $x(\cdot)$ solves (\ref{eq:managerproblem}) given $p(\cdot)$ and $\{m_{i}(\cdot)\}_{i=1}^n$, and each $m_i(\cdot)$ solves (\ref{eq:investorproblem}) given $p(\cdot)$ and $x(\cdot)$.

\section{Equilibrium} \label{sec:eqm}

In this section, we search for a linear equilibrium under transparency ($\omega_s=0$), in which each trader's market order $x$ is linear in her skill, $s$. Proofs for the analytical results are provided in the \hyperref[app:appendix]{Online Appendix}.

%===========================================
\subsection{Trading Strategy} \label{ss:conjecture}
Conjecture and later verify that the manager's equilibrium trading strategy is
\begin{equation}
 x(s)=\beta s,\label{x_conj}
\end{equation}
where $\beta>0$ is an endogenous constant determined later. Conjecture \eqref{x_conj} states that the manager buys (sells) the asset if her $s$ is positive (negative), where the order size is proportional to her skill $|s|$; the more skilled the manager, the more aggressively she trades.\footnote{This conjecture follows from the original Kyle model where an informed trader trades based on the difference between her belief and that of the market maker, i.e., $ \mathrm{E}[\delta|s]-\mathrm{E}[\delta] = s$.} The scale factor $\beta$ is referred to as the {\it trading intensity}.

%===========================================
\subsection{Execution Price} \label{ss:price}
Market makers decide on the price of each asset by extracting information about the asset's payoff from the aggregate order flow, $x+u$. Anticipating the trading strategy \eqref{x_conj}, the price in equation \eqref{p} can be rewritten as
\begin{equation}
p=\bar{\delta}+\lambda(x+u),\label{eq:p}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
\lambda\equiv \frac{\beta \omega_{s}}{\beta^{2} \omega_{s}+\omega_{u}}\label{lambda}
\end{equation}
is ``Kyle's lambda,'' a measure of the trader's price impact.\footnote{From \eqref{p}, we have $p=\mathrm{E}[\delta|x+u]=\mathrm{E}[\mathrm{E}[\delta|x+u,s]|x+u]=\mathrm{E}[\bar{\delta}+s|x+u]$. Since market makers believe that the manager follows \eqref{x_conj}, $s$ and $x+u$ are both normally distributed from market makers' perspective. Thus, $p=\bar{\delta}+\mathrm{E}[s]+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}[s,x+u]}{\mathrm{Var}[x+u]}(x+u-\mathrm{E}[x+u])=\bar{\delta}+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}[s,\beta s+u]}{\mathrm{Var}[\beta s+u]}(x+u-\mathrm{E}[\beta s+u])=\bar{\delta}+\frac{\beta \omega_{s}}{\beta^{2} \omega_{s}+\omega_{u}}(x+u)$.} Following the literature, we define a market to be {\it liquid} if $\lambda$ is small.

%===========================================
\subsection{Investment Delegation} \label{ss:delegation}
Investor $i$'s optimization problem in (\ref{eq:investorproblem}) is rewritten as 
\begin{equation}
    \max_{m_i} \frac{m_i}{M}(1-\phi)\mathrm{E}\left[\pi|x+e\right]-\phi m_i,\label{eq:investorproblem-transparent}
\end{equation}
leading to the following optimal delegation:
\begin{equation}
    m_{i} = \left(\frac{1-\phi}{\phi}\mathrm{E}[\pi|x+e]\sum_{j\neq i}m_j\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}-\sum_{j\neq i}m_j.\label{eq:mi}
\end{equation}
The cost of delegating investment is linear in the dollar value of the stake, as represented by the second term of (\ref{eq:investorproblem-transparent}), while the marginal benefit of an additional stake is diminishing, as is captured by the first term. Hence, given the total fund allocation by other investors, $\sum_{j\neq i}m_j$, investor $i$'s optimal delegation is determined by the FOC as (\ref{eq:mi}).

We focus on the symmetric equilibrium where all investors provide the same amount of capital to the fund. By imposing $m_i = m_j$ for all $i,j\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}$, (\ref{eq:mi}) is reduced to 
\begin{equation}
    m = \frac{n-1}{n^2}\frac{1-\phi}{\phi}\mathrm{E}[\pi|x+e].\label{eq:symmetricm}
\end{equation}
It also yields the total asset under management (AUM) of the fund as 
\begin{equation}
    M \equiv nm = \frac{n-1}{n}\frac{1-\phi}{\phi}\mathrm{E}[\pi|x+e].\label{eq:M}
\end{equation}
In what follows, we denote $\frac{n-1}{n}\frac{1-\phi}{\phi}=\theta$. Individual and the total fund provisions are proportional to the expected value of trading profits conditional on the fund information disseminated to investors, $\mathrm{E}[\pi|x+e]$. This is a natural consequence of the performance-based fee structure, as it makes the per-unit return of fund delegation proportional to the trading profit. %As the conditional expected profit depends on the fund information and the trading strategy, $x$, it influences the manager's incentive in deciding on the trading strategy.

By incorporating the trading strategy in (\ref{x_conj}) and the pricing rule in (\ref{p}), both the trading quantity, $x$, and the profit margin (i.e., informational advantages), $\mathrm{E}[\delta-p|s]$, are proportional to the realized skill level, $|s|$, making the profit a quadratic function of $s$. When the fund information is perfectly transparent ($\omega_{e}=0$), investors back out the realized skill level directly from the signal, $s = \frac{x}{\beta}$, so that the conditional expectation leads to the following estimate:
\begin{equation}
      \mathrm{E}\left[\pi|x+e\right] = (1-\lambda \beta)\beta\mathrm{E}[s^2|s=x/\beta]=\frac{1-\lambda\beta}{\beta}x^2.\label{eq:exp_pi_transparent}
\end{equation}
Together with the total fund provision in (\ref{eq:M}), the above estimate implies that the delegated investment may generate an additional incentive for the manager to control her trading strategy, $x$. Namely, the manger can positively influence the total fund flow by inflating the trading quantity, as it persuades investors of the manager's high realized skill level.

\subsection{Manager's Optimization} \label{ss:optimization}
By incorporating the total fund flow ([\ref{eq:M}] and [\ref{eq:exp_pi_transparent}]), the manager's problem in (\ref{eq:managerproblem}) is re-written as
\begin{equation}
    \max_{x} \phi\left[sx - \lambda x^2   + \theta\frac{1-\lambda\beta}{\beta}x^2\right],\label{eq:managerproblem_transparent}
\end{equation}
where the first two terms represents the fee income from the trading profit and are essentially identical to the original \citet{kyle1985continuous} model. The third term arises due to the fund inflow and is determined by the quadratic size of risky investments. By incorporating the pricing rule (\ref{p}) and taking the FOC, the optimal trading strategy involves
\begin{equation}
    x = \frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta}{\lambda\beta - \theta(1-\lambda\beta)}s.\label{eq:x=Fbeta}
\end{equation}
This strategy is consistent with our guess in (\ref{x_conj}) when $\beta = \frac{1}{2}\frac{\beta}{\lambda\beta - \theta(1-\lambda\beta)}$, leading to the following lemma:
\begin{lemma}\label{lem:transparent}
    When the fund information is transparent ($\omega_s=0$), the equilibrium trading strategy and the pricing rules are specified by 
    \begin{equation}
        \beta_{0} = \sqrt{(1+2\theta)\frac{\omega_{u}}{\omega_{s}}},\ \lambda _0 = \frac{1}{2(1+\theta)}\sqrt{(1+2\theta)\frac{\omega_{s}}{\omega_{u}}}.\label{eq:eqm_trade_transparent}
    \end{equation}
\end{lemma}
As is evident from problem (\ref{eq:managerproblem_transparent}), parameter $\theta$ captures the manager's additional incentive to control her risky investment arising from the fund delegation, and the equilibrium reduces to the original \citet{kyle1985continuous} as $\theta\rightarrow0$. This case highlights the manager's tradeoff in deciding on her trading intensity: while she seeks to exploit her informational advantage by trading more intensively, it reveals information to market makers and raises the price impact, reducing the trading profit. Without fund delegation ($\theta=0$), the equilibrium $\beta$ is determined to balance these competing effects. Conversely, when $\theta$ increases, the share of the total fund flow becomes larger in the manager's utility, and she becomes more concerned about it. As shown in (\ref{eq:M}) and (\ref{eq:exp_pi_transparent}), inflating the trading quantity benefits the manager by persuading investors of high $|s|$ and inducing a larger fund flow. Therefore, $\beta$ becomes an increasing function of $\theta$. With this $\hat{s}$, the trader's objective in equation \eqref{max_prob} can be written as a simple quadratic function of $x$, as presented in the following lemma.

\begin{lemma}[Trader's problem] \label{lemma:maximization} Each trader's maximization problem is
\begin{equation}
\max_{x}\ s x-\lambda x^{2}+\theta\xi^{2}\left(\gamma^{2}x^{2}+2\gamma(1-\gamma)s x\right).\label{max_prob_2}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

The first term of equation \eqref{max_prob_2} is the trader's expected trading revenue. The second term represents the trading cost resulting from her price impact, which is increasing in the size of her trade, $|x|$. The third term corresponds to her reputation benefit, which also increases with $|x|$ because she can potentially inflate her reputation $|\hat{s}|$ by placing a large order (either buy or sell) and influencing the price that the recruiter observes.

The unique solution to \eqref{max_prob_2} (given $\beta$) is
\begin{equation}
x=F(\beta)s,\label{x}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
F(\beta)\equiv\frac{1+2\theta\xi^{2}\gamma(1-\gamma)}{2\left(\lambda-\theta\xi^{2}\gamma^{2}\right)}.\label{F}
\end{equation}
$F(\cdot)$ is a nonlinear function of $\beta$ because $\lambda$, $\xi$, and $\gamma$ are functions of $\beta$.

\subsection{Equilibrium} \label{ss:equilibrium}
Each trader optimally chooses \eqref{x} given that the market maker and the recruiter believe that she follows strategy \eqref{x_conj}. In equilibrium, their beliefs must be correct, that is, \eqref{x_conj} and \eqref{x} must be consistent with each other, requiring the following condition:
\begin{equation}
\beta=F(\beta).\label{fixed_point}
\end{equation}
That is, the equilibrium levels of $\beta$ are determined at the fixed points of $F(\cdot)$. Having identified $\beta$, the equilibrium levels of $x$ and $p$ are determined by equations \eqref{x_conj} and \eqref{eq:p}, respectively. In what follows, we first illustrate the determination of equilibrium $\beta$ through a numerical example, followed by an analytical proposition that formally establishes the equilibria.

\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Impact of reputation concerns.}}}

To see the intuition behind the different equilibrium patterns presented in Figure \ref{fig:exog}, note that the trader faces two conflicting motives when trading. On the one hand, she seeks to avoid trading too aggressively on her private information, as doing so would move the price excessively and reduce her trading profits. As in \citet{kyle1985continuous}, this motive leads the trader to decrease $\beta$. On the other hand, she wishes to trade intensively to signal her high skill to the recruiter, aiming to establish a strong reputation. This motive encourages her to increase $\beta$. In summary, she faces a tradeoff between ``hiding'' her skill from the market maker and ``showing it off'' to the recruiter. The degree of reputation concern, $\theta$, governs this tradeoff.

%To see the intuition for the different equilibrium patterns presented in Figure \ref{fig:exog}, recall that each trader faces two conflicting motives when trading. On the one hand, trading intensively would cause a severe adverse selection, leading the market maker to impose a large price impact that erodes the trader's profit. As in \citet{kyle1985continuous}, this makes the trader decrease $\beta$. On the other hand, intensive trading helps establish a high reputation, as it signals that the trader is of high skill, thereby encouraging her to increase $\beta$. The degree of reputation concern, $\theta$, governs this tradeoff. 

For small $\theta$ (panel (a)), the hiding motive dominates the showing-off motive, leading to a unique low-$\beta$ equilibrium. In this equilibrium, the recruiter believes that the trader's trading intensity is relatively low. Knowing this, the trader could inflate her reputation by placing an order larger than what the recruiter anticipates. However, she would refrain from doing so because it would cause a large price impact, reducing her trading profits. Consequently, the equilibrium $\beta$ is close to $\beta_{\theta=0}$, meaning that it is a ``Kyle-like equilibrium." 

For large $\theta$ (panel (b)), the showing-off motive prevails, resulting in a unique high-$\beta$ equilibrium. In this equilibrium, the trader is willing to move the price substantially, undermining her profits to appear skilled. Importantly, the recruiter's learning is not manipulated, as his belief about the trader's trading strategy is correct. Nonetheless, the trader places a large order because doing otherwise would induce the recruiter to incorrectly estimate that she is less skilled, thereby deteriorating her reputation. She trades aggressively solely to conform to the recruiter's belief. To emphasize this mechanism, we call this equilibrium the ``reputation-driven equilibrium."

For intermediate levels of $\theta$ (panel (c)), there is no clear dominance between the two motives, hence both the low-$\beta$ and the high-$\beta$ equilibria are supported. Each equilibrium is associated with a self-fulfilling belief of the market maker and the recruiter.

\begin{proposition}[Equilibrium characterization]\label{prop:eqm}
There exists a critical value of the skill potential, $\tilde{\omega}_{s}>0$, and two thresholds for reputation concern, $\theta_{L}$ and $\theta_{H}$ $(\geq\theta_{L})$, such that, $\theta\geq \theta_{H}$ leads to a unique high-$\beta$ equilibrium, while $\theta\leq\theta_{L}$ leads to a unique low-$\beta$ equilibrium. Furthermore,
\begin{enumerate}[topsep=2.5pt, label = (\roman*)]
 \item if $\omega_{s}\leq \tilde{\omega}_{s}$ then $\theta_{H}=\theta_{L}$, hence the equilibrium is unique; and
 \item if $\omega_{s}> \tilde{\omega}_{s}$ then $\theta_{H}>\theta_{L}$, and $\theta\in(\theta_{L},\theta_{H})$ leads to multiple equilibria.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Impact of skill potential.}}}
Proposition \ref{prop:eqm} and Figure \ref{fig:theta} indicate that, for a given $\theta$, a low skill potential $\omega_{s}$ supports only the high-$\beta$ equilibrium, while increases in $\omega_{s}$ can generate the low-$\beta$ equilibrium, potentially leading to multiple equilibria.\footnote{Lemma \ref{lem:theta_s} in Online Appendix \ref{proofendogenous} analytically characterizes the upward-sloping $\theta_{H}$ and $\theta_{L}$ in relation to $\omega_{s}$, as presented in Figure \ref{fig:theta}.} To understand intuition, the tradeoff between hiding and showing-off motives, as explained above, is key. 
%On the one hand, she seeks to avoid trading too aggressively on her private information, as doing so would move the price excessively and reduce her trading profits. On the other hand, she wishes to trade intensively to show off her skill to the recruiter. 
When $\omega_{s}$ is small, the market maker anticipates that the trader has low skill and, due to limited adverse selection, sets a low price impact $\lambda$. In this case, the trader’s hiding motive is less important. Consequently, the showing-off motive prevails, prompting her to trade aggressively and leading to the high-$\beta$ equilibrium. Conversely, when $\omega_{s}$ is large, the market maker sets a high $\lambda$, and the hiding motive becomes more influential, making the low-$\beta$ equilibrium more likely to be realized.

Overall, the type of equilibrium depends on the combination of $\theta$ and $\omega_{s}$, as illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:theta}. Small $\theta$ and large $\omega_{s}$, both of which amplify the trader's hiding motive, lead to the low-$\beta$ equilibrium. Conversely, large $\theta$ and small $\omega_{s}$, which both intensify her showing-off motive, result in the high-$\beta$ equilibrium. However, with a combination of large $\theta$ and large $\omega_{s}$, the result is ambiguous, as the two motives offset each other. In this case, either the high-$\beta$ or the low-$\beta$ equilibrium may be realized, depending on the market's self-fulfilling beliefs.

%When $\omega_{s}$ is small ($\omega_{s} \leq \tilde{\omega}_{s}$), the market maker anticipates that the trader has low skill and, due to limited adverse selection, sets a low price impact $\lambda$. In this case, the trader’s hiding motive is less important. Thus, with the showing-off motive prevailing, she trades intensively, resulting in the reputation-driven, high-$\beta$ equilibrium. Conversely, when $\omega_{s}$ is large ($\omega_{s} > \tilde{\omega}_{s}$), the market maker sets a high $\lambda$, making the hiding motive more influential. In such a case, the level of $\theta$, representing the relative strength of the showing-off motive, determines the equilibrium type. If $\theta$ is high ($\theta \geq \theta_{H}$), the showing-off motive dominates, yielding a unique high-$\beta$ equilibrium. If $\theta$ is low ($\theta \leq \theta_{L}$), the hiding motive prevails, leading to a unique low-$\beta$ equilibrium. If $\theta$ falls within a middle range ($\theta \in (\theta_{L}, \theta_{H})$), neither motive clearly dominates, so both high-$\beta$ and low-$\beta$ equilibria emerge, depending on the market's self-fulfilling beliefs.

%To understand the intuition, recall that a trader faces two conflicting motives: trading profit and reputation benefit. With this reduced sensitivity of price and trading profit to trading behavior, the trader’s concern with trading profit diminishes, allowing the reputation benefit to become the dominant factor and resulting in the reputation-driven equilibrium. As skill potential rises, the market maker becomes more reactive to order flow. A high price impact then compels the trader to weigh both conflicting motives, leading to multiple equilibria.

\paragraph{\normalfont{\it{Equilibrium comparison.}}}
Comparing the two stable equilibria, does the trader perform better in one of them? What are the implications for market liquidity, asset prices, and learning about asset-selection skills?

\begin{corollary}[High-$\beta$ vs. low-$\beta$ equilibrium]\label{cor:high_vs_low}
In the high-$\beta$ equilibrium, relative to the low-$\beta$ equilibrium,
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item \label{item:1} the market liquidity is higher, that is, $\lambda$ is lower;
\item \label{item:2} the trader's expected trading profit given the realized skill, $\mathrm{E}[(\delta-p)x|s]=\frac{\beta\omega_{u}s^{2}}{\beta^{2} \omega_{s}+\omega_{u}}$, is lower;
\item \label{item:3} the price informativeness, $\tau\equiv\frac{1}{\mathrm{Var}[\delta|p]}=\frac{\beta^{2} \omega_{s}+\omega_{u}}{\omega_{\delta}(\beta^{2} \omega_{s}+\omega_{u})+\omega_{u} \omega_{s}}$, is higher;
\item \label{item:4} the price volatility, $\mathrm{Var}[p]=\frac{\beta^{2}\omega_{s}^{2}}{\beta^{2} \omega_{s}+\omega_{u}}$, is higher; and
\item \label{item:5} the recruiter's estimate of $s$ is more precise, that is, the posterior variance of $s$, $\hat{\omega}_{s}=\frac{\omega_{s}\omega_{u}\omega_{\delta}}{\omega_{s}(\beta^{2}\omega_{\delta}+\omega_{u})+\omega_{u}\omega_{\delta}}$, is lower.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

Statement \ref{item:1} of Corollary \ref{cor:high_vs_low} holds because price impact $\lambda$ is decreasing in $\beta$. Since the high-$\beta$ equilibrium emerges only if $\theta$ is relatively large, the statement also implies that a higher reputation concern leads to higher market liquidity. The intuition is as follows. Relative to the low-$\beta$ equilibrium, in the high-$\beta$ equilibrium the trader trades more aggressively and thus reveals more private information on $s$ to the market maker. From the market maker's perspective, the information is ``exaggerated'' in that the order size is too large relative to the true $|s|$. Thus, the market maker attempts to partially ``undo'' the trader's information revelation by incorporating less of it into the price. This mechanism results in a reduced price impact. %{\color{red}[Revisit this---see Kyle 85 model explanation in AP notes.]}

The intuition for statement \ref{item:2} is closely related to that for statement \ref{item:1}. In the high-$\beta$ equilibrium, the trader earns a lower trading profit due to a smaller informational advantage over the market maker. This result may appear inconsistent with statement \ref{item:1} because, ceteris paribus, a lower price impact is typically associated with a higher trading profit. However, the trader's order size $|x|$ in the high-$\beta$ equilibrium is so large that the {\it overall} price impact (i.e., $\lambda$ multiplied by $|x|$) is larger than that in the low-$\beta$ equilibrium. Overall, the trader moves the price excessively and earns a lower trading profit in this equilibrium.
%\footnote{Our numerical result, not presented in the paper but available upon request, indicates that the trader's overall expected utility, including the reputation benefit, is also lower in the high-$\beta$ equilibrium.} 
Due to her aggressive trading, more information about $s$---which is informative about $\delta$---is incorporated into the price in the high-$\beta$ equilibrium, supporting statement \ref{item:3}.

Statement \ref{item:4} follows, again, from the trader's aggressive trading in the high-$\beta$ equilibrium. Specifically, she places a large buy (sell) order when $s>0$ ($s<0$), even if $|s|$ is small. This causes a large upward (downward) pressure on $p$, leading to greater price variability. This result aligns with the insight of \citet{guerrieri2012fund} that reputation concerns amplify price volatility, although their mechanism differs from ours.\footnote{\citet{guerrieri2012fund} argue that bond price volatility increases because fund managers demand a premium to offset the risk of reputation damage in the event of default.}

In the high-$\beta$ equilibrium, the trader reveals more information about $s$, thereby improving the precision of the recruiter's estimation (statement \ref{item:5}). This makes the reputation benefit more responsive to skill, as the recruiter can assess it more accurately.
